Public Transport in Tunisia: Daily Disintegration

Yassine Nabli
2025-12-08    |   


The dilapidated and overcrowded public bus and light rail stations in central Tunis serve as strong evidence of the dark fate that the public transport sector is facing in a congested urban space where passage is becoming increasingly obstructed. The public transport fleet is disintegrating day by day, with most of its components turning into piles of scrap beyond repair. The sector is dying a dramatic death, not only in the middle of the capital and major urban centers but also in areas and regions already suffering from inequitable access to public transport. This gradual demise is causing many social, economic, and daily living predicaments that have transformed the country into a discouraging, unsafe, and exhausting space that paralyzes movement, especially for the most vulnerable groups.

 

Amidst the disintegration of this public service, phenomena reflecting the escalation of the crisis have emerged. These include the “moto taxi”, which is now used by many Tunisians – especially in the center of the capital – due to the recession of public transport. The private taxi and shared taxi have also become essential modes of inner-city transport for most segments of society, causing individuals and households to spend more on transport at the expense of other essential needs. Meanwhile, many segments – especially the middle and lower-middle classes – are increasingly resorting to purchasing cars, relying on bank loans and incurring additional expenses on fuel, insurance, and fees.

 

Many indicators, statistics, and reports – especially official ones – corroborate the chronic dysfunction now afflicting Tunisia’s public transport sector. These include the enormous decline in the size of the fleet, the meager resources allocated to transport in the public budget, the rising expenses that individuals and households incur on transport services, the financial deficits of public transport companies, and the increasing prominence of alternative modes of transport. This article examines these indicators and their dimensions and attempts to construct a picture of the public policies that the state and the current political system are following with regard to the transport sector.

 

A Public Transport Fleet Out of Service

 

During a hearing held by a parliamentary committee in mid-February this year, Minister of Transport Rashid Ameri attempted to present some indicators concerning the public transport fleet, albeit without offering a comprehensive national inventory, especially for the regions. He stated that the number of buses operated by the Tunis Transport Company (Transtu) had fallen to approximately 400 and the number of rail cars had fallen to approximately 40.[1] Two years earlier, on 16 February 2023, Transtu presented more detailed data on the dire condition of its fleet: between 2010 and 2022, the number of roaming buses declined from 744 to 437, the number of rail cars declined from 118 to 57, and the number of trains on the Tunis Marine line declined from 11 to just five.[2]

 

The same data indicated that delays in completing annual replacement programs had led to the accumulation of 277 buses meeting the criteria for replacement at the end of December 2021. Two hundred and thirty-nine buses were then withdrawn from service in October 2022. This has led to the wear and tear of new buses due to overuse, with 81% of mileage being completed by 32% of all buses. Moreover, the unavailability of spare parts for the light rail fleet has negatively impacted its readiness and ability to meet increasing demands, compounded by delays in the refurbishment of a significant portion of the fleet due to high costs and insufficient funding. Delays in implementing procurement programs have also resulted in an aging public transport fleet, with the average vehicle age reaching 12 years and seven months for buses, 27 years and 11 months for rail cars, and 44 years and 11 months for rail cars on the Tunis Marine line.

 

The most recent Ministry of Transport statistics on the public transport fleet in the regions and between cities were published in 2019. They indicated that in 2017, there were approximately 2,567 buses operated by regional transport companies and 72 buses operated by the National Company of Interurban Transport (SNTRI). Even though this number of buses distributed across approximately 23 governorates was already very low, it has declined since 2017. This decline is logical as the state has no clear vision for developing regional public transport (especially in the interior regions), and there is no indication that the regional transport fleet is being renewed or that clear alternatives to address its erosion are being developed. 

 

In the aforementioned parliamentary hearing, the transport minister merely mentioned that 300 buses would be distributed among all regions before September 2025, without clarifying the resources allocated for these buses or the sources from which they would be procured. Meanwhile, local media reported from official sources that last April, Transtu received 165 used buses retired from French lines as part of a donation agreement it signed with the Paris Independent Transport Agency and Île-de-France Mobilités. Tunisia had signed two previous deals to purchase used buses from France, the first in 2015-2017 at a cost of TND9.5 million [1TND = USD0.35] and the second in 2023-2024 for 300 buses at a cost of TND16 million.[3]

 

The continued recourse – from 2015 to today – to secondhand French buses no longer fit for service in their country of origin has not solved the transport fleet conundrum in Tunisia. Rather, these buses have become an additional burden, with some having already been taken out of service. Recently, the Ministry of Transport announced the procurement of 300 buses from China without providing details about the cost of the deal and the nature of the resources allocated for it (loans or domestic resources?). These band-aid solutions involving the procurement of used buses come at a time when other, more efficient and higher-capacity modes of transport, such as light rail and high-speed trains, are being neglected. These modes are suffering from gradual disintegration and insufficient development of rail networks, with some even being shut down.

 

The Transport Companies’ Deficit and the Application of Austerity

 

The public transport companies in the various regions of the country are facing financial difficulties and an inability to repair their buses (some of which are 25 years old), in addition to accumulated debts, a lack of maintenance resources, and staff shortages. Transtu previously announced that in 2022, its debts had reached close to TND1.9 billion, including TND230 million owed to banks and spare-part suppliers. Similarly, on 10 March 2023, the Regional Transport Company of Sfax (SORETRAS) announced that it was experiencing a range of difficulties that “prevent it from maintaining its fiscal balances because of the volume of accumulated losses and scale of its debts (long-term, medium-term, and short-term) compared to the size of its property and assets”. The company explained that “total equity stood at approximately negative TND109 million at the end of 2022 because of the volume of losses from year to year, which were estimated at TND122 million at that time”.[4]

 

The Regional Transport Company of Medenine (SRTM, in the southeast) is facing similar difficulties. In August 2024, transport syndicates in the Tunisian General Labor Union announced that the company was suffering from an aging bus fleet, with the average vehicle age reaching 25 years. This issue has led to “a rise in repair and maintenance costs and a decline in safety standards”. The same source mentioned that “the company’s accumulation of debts to suppliers has led to an irregular supply of the fuel and spare parts that the company needs to operate its fleet normally”.[5]

 

Likewise, a report on public facilities annexed to the 2025 Financial Law revealed that many public transport companies are suffering from financial deficits. These include the National Tunisian Railway Company (SNCFT), which recorded a net loss of TND199.8 million in 2022.[6] As for SNTRI, it has been suffering from an escalating deficit for approximately 10 years. A report issued by the Court of Accounts in 2016 indicates that the company’s accumulated losses had exceeded four times its capital. The report added that this situation has rendered the company unable to invest in new buses, pay outstanding debts, and pay its staff on time.[7]

 

The dramatic deficit from which most public transport companies are suffering reflects the state’s abdication of its role in developing this sector. Part of the state’s discourse is attempting to divert the blame to fare-evading travelers or teenagers who engage in “vandalism” on public transport. Another part of this discourse is pushing for total privatization of the transport sector and the transfer of public facilities to private investors. Yet the structural crisis clearly lies elsewhere.All the official strategies, reports, and plans during the last five years, if not earlier, indicate a total lack of structural strategic planning to address three urgent needs: restructuring the public transport companies, renewing the transport fleet beyond makeshift deals, and developing transport networks (especially rail networks) in a decentralized manner that accounts for the particular circumstances of marginal groups and the most isolated and stagnant regions.

 

In keeping with its general political discourse, the current authority is striving to explain the public transport crises as a legacy of the “democratic transition decade” or the decades that preceded it, as well as a reflection of incompetent management. In this manner, President Kais Saied’s regime is attempting to shirk its political responsibility to engage in reform and strategic planning. There is no doubt that the transport sector is suffering from mismanagement and poor administration and is dragging along an old legacy of neglect. However, the current authority is pushing it toward the abyss, particularly by continuing to apply a strict austerity policy in the name of “self-sufficiency” while simultaneously relying on loans and donations to import used buses, as we explained earlier. 

 

Austerity is evident in the funds allocated to the Ministry of Transport,[8] which oversees approximately 29 public institutions across all realms of transport. Its budget for this year stood at around TND1billion, which is only 2.4% larger than last year – a meager increase in comparison to the inflation rate, which reached 5.4% last May. Moreover, these resources, which constitute only 1.8% of all public spending planned in the budget, are not commensurate with the short-term and medium-term projects presented by the ministry, which include “establishing new light rail lines”, “upgrading stations”, and “accelerating the pace at which projects are completed”.[9]

 

Clearly, there is a real problem of resources that the current authority is unwilling to acknowledge or look for new alternatives to address. Instead, it is implementing austerity policies and a few temporary solutions in practice. Meanwhile, in its discourse, it is promoting itself as an unconventional authority striving to rely on domestic resources whose size, nature, and source it does not always disclose.

 

The Collapse of Public Transport: Alternatives Between Legal Grey Areas and Car Imports

 

The recession of “mass” public transport (buses, trains, and light rail) has left many gaps now filled by other choices such as “moto taxis”, private taxis, shared taxis, and intercity taxis. Meanwhile, a rapidly increasing number of Tunisians are resorting to purchasing cars through the “popular car” program or car-import dealers.

 

The “moto taxi” is a newly emerging phenomenon concentrated in central Tunis. Although the Ministry of Transport and law-enforcement authorities still consider it an informal mode of transport operating outside the law, commuters have widely adopted it, especially because of public transport’s irregularity and absence from many routes. The “moto taxi”, despite its inexpensiveness and dangerousness, fulfills many people’s need to reach their workplaces on time or complete administrative errands. Moreover, the private taxi is no longer a luxury option but a basic necessity. The most recent tally published by the Ministry of Transport revealed that by December 2019, private taxis numbered approximately 32,183 and shared taxis numbered 2,701.[10] Recourse to private cars is also increasing among many social groups, especially those with access to loans. In previous statements,[11] National Chamber for Car Manufacturers and Importers President Ibrahim Dabash did not hide that “the failure of public transport has boosted Tunisians’ turn toward private cars”. He said that the annual volume of car imports stands at 60,000 cars – a number that he deemed insufficient to meet the market demand for 80,000 cars per year. He added that by early 2025, 100,000 applications for a “popular car” had been lodged. Media reports in 2018 indicated that in December 2017, the number of vehicles registered with the Technical Agency of Land Transport stood at approximately 2.1 million, including 1.3 million private cars.[12] Logically, this number would have increased over the following eight years, given Dabash’s statements about the pressure in the annual demand for private cars.

 

Generally, recourse to irregular modes of public transport or private cars increases individuals’ and families’ commuting expenses. Although there are no clear and precise statistics on the growth of Tunisians’ spending on transport, figures published by the National Institute for Statistics in 2022 indicate that in 2021, Tunisians spent an average of TND5,468 in total throughout the year, of which approximately 6.9% (TND375) went to commuting.[13] This figure does not reflect the growth of household and individual spending on transport in reality because it is calculated only on the basis of the aforementioned average individual spending.

 

The current disintegration of public transport is leading to a shift in the structure of transport in Tunisia, which will become more dependent on taxis, private cars, and certain other modes operating in legal grey areas such as the “moto taxi”. This shift will lead to a change in the social and political perception of this service, which will cease to be seen as a basic right and become a “privilege” that is difficult to access, especially for the most economically vulnerable groups.
