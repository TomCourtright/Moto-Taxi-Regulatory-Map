Legal Basis for Habal-Habal (Motorcycle Taxi) Operations in the Philippines

As of my latest update (June 2024). This is general information, not legal advice.

Executive summary

  • Under current national law, two-wheeled motorcycles are not recognized as public utility vehicles (PUVs) for passenger carriage. Operating a motorcycle “for hire” as a passenger service (a.k.a. habal-habal / motorcycle taxi) is generally unlawful unless covered by a limited DOTr-led pilot program.
  • Why: The Public Service Act (Commonwealth Act No. 146) requires a Certificate of Public Convenience (CPC) from the LTFRB for any public transport service, and the Land Transportation and Traffic Code (RA 4136) plus implementing rules do not create a passenger-carrying PUV class for two-wheel motorcycles. The LTO also does not register ordinary motorcycles as “for hire” passenger vehicles.
  • The exception: Since 2019, the DOTr Technical Working Group (TWG) Motorcycle-Taxi Pilot has allowed a time-bounded, data-gathering program (with capped fleets, fixed corridors, and strict conditions) for select platform operators (notably Angkas, JoyRide, and Move It). This is not a permanent legalization; it’s an administrative study pending legislation.
  • Local ordinances cannot “legalize” motorcycle taxis as PUVs. LGUs can regulate traffic and issue business permits, but franchising for two-wheeled passenger service remains a national function (LTFRB). LGU franchising powers under the Local Government Code generally cover tricycles with sidecars, not two-wheel motorcycles.
  • Enforcement: Outside the pilot’s scope, motorcycle taxis are treated as “colorum” operations, subject to fines and vehicle impoundment under joint DOTr/LTFRB/LTO rules.
  • Safety & liability: Riders and passengers must comply with national helmet and back-riding safety laws. CTPL insurance is mandatory for registration, but coverage is limited; platforms typically carry additional personal accident insurance. Contract and tort liability principles (common carrier standards vs. private carriage) shape risk exposure.

The national legal framework

1) Public Service Act & franchising

  • Commonwealth Act No. 146 (Public Service Act) requires a CPC from the LTFRB (created by EO 202 [1987]) to operate any public transport service “for compensation.”
  • Consequence: A vehicle type must be eligible for franchising. The LTFRB’s PUV classes historically cover jeepneys, buses, UV Express, taxis (four-wheel), etc.—not two-wheel motorcycles for passenger hire.

2) Land Transportation and Traffic Code & LTO classification

  • RA 4136 governs registration, licensing, and road rules. While it recognizes motorcycles as motor vehicles, it does not create a “motorcycle-for-hire (passenger)” category.
  • LTO practice: ordinary motorcycles are registered as private (or government). There is no routine “for hire” passenger registration for two-wheel motorcycles—hence the legal gap for motorcycle taxis.

3) Local Government Code (LGU powers)

  • RA 7160 authorizes LGUs to regulate traffic, issue business permits, and, by long-standing practice and DILG guidance, franchise tricycles (three-wheel with sidecar) for local routes.
  • But: LGUs cannot confer CPC-equivalent authority on two-wheel motorcycles to operate as passenger PUVs. Franchising for public land transport remains with LTFRB.
  • Bottom line: LGUs may pass safety or permitting ordinances (e.g., parking, terminals, vests), but they cannot override national franchising law to “legalize” two-wheel habal-habal as PUVs.

The DOTr Motorcycle-Taxi Pilot (administrative exception)

Nature: An executive pilot / study begun in 2019, repeatedly extended to gather safety, capacity, and regulatory data pending Congress action.

Key features (typical across iterations):

  • Operators admitted by DOTr TWG (e.g., Angkas, JoyRide, Move It).
  • Capped fleets and service areas (e.g., Metro Manila, Metro Cebu, and other specified cities subject to period guidelines).
  • Rider qualifications: valid license, clean record, age/experience minimums, training and skills verification, roadworthiness checks.
  • Vehicle requirements: displacement/age caps set by TWG versions; safety gear (standard helmet, reflective vest/ID, phone mounts positioned safely, etc.).
  • Data & compliance: trip logging/telemetry, incident reporting, periodic audits, and cooperation with regulators.
  • Fares: government-approved / capped during the pilot; surge or dynamic pricing restricted per TWG rules.
  • Insurance: CTPL for registration plus platform-provided personal accident coverage for riders/passengers.
  • Enforcement carve-out: While inside pilot parameters, participating riders are not treated as colorum; outside the parameters (out-of-area, beyond caps, non-participant), enforcement applies.

Important: The pilot does not amend statutes. It is a temporary, policy-testing mechanism relying on DOTr’s administrative powers—not a permanent legal basis.

Where legislation stands (big picture)

  • Since 2019, both chambers have filed bills to explicitly legalize “motorcycle-for-hire” as a PUV class, typically proposing:

    • Amending RA 4136 (and related laws) to recognize two-wheeled passenger service;
    • Empowering LTFRB to grant CPCs to motorcycle taxi operators and set safety/fare standards;
    • Mandating training, inspections, equipment, and insurance; and
    • Imposing penalties for non-compliance.

  • As of mid-2024, no nationwide permanent law had been enacted; the activity remained under the pilot and local traffic regulation (not franchising). (Status may have changed after that date.)

Safety, equipment, and conduct (still applicable)

Even outside PUV status, all motorcycles and riders must comply with national road-safety laws:

  • RA 10054 (Motorcycle Helmet Act of 2009) – Standard protective helmets for rider and backrider.
  • RA 10666 (Children’s Safety on Motorcycles Act of 2015) – Limits back-riding of small children on public roads except in narrow, safety-qualified scenarios.
  • RA 10913 (Anti-Distracted Driving Act) – No device use in a manner that obstructs view/operation; phones must be mounted safely; no texting/browsing while in motion.
  • Traffic codes (RA 4136 and LTO rules) – Lane discipline, speed limits, lighting, number plates, modification restrictions, etc.

Platforms in the pilot typically add:

  • Rider training & accreditation, periodic re-checks;
  • Zero-tolerance policies for alcohol/drugs;
  • Incident/near-miss reporting and app-based safety features (SOS, trip-sharing);
  • Quality control (star ratings, suspensions).

Insurance & liability

  • CTPL (Compulsory Third-Party Liability): Required for registration under the Insurance Code. Coverage is limited and may not fully protect paying passengers in a for-hire context.

  • Additional coverage: Pilot-admitted platforms typically carry Personal Accident Insurance for riders and passengers with stated limits.

  • Liability theories:

    • Where a service is legally a PUV, common-carrier standards apply (extraordinary diligence).
    • Because two-wheel motorcycle taxis are not yet a permanent PUV class, courts may analyze liability under contract/tort, considering pilot rules, platform terms, and general negligence standards.

  • Practical tip: Riders should confirm policy scope (who is insured, limits, exclusions) and keep documentation handy; platforms should maintain proof of cover and clear claims protocols.

Enforcement landscape

  • Outside pilot coverage, motorcycle taxis are treated as unauthorized (“colorum”) public transport.
  • Grounds: operating for compensation without a CPC (Public Service Act) and vehicle type not franchised as a PUV; LTO registration not “for hire.”
  • Sanctions (per joint DOTr/LTFRB/LTO issuances, e.g., JAO 2014-01 and successors): significant fines, impoundment, and possible license/permit action.
  • Local enforcement: LGUs may apprehend for traffic offenses and ordinance breaches (e.g., terminals, vests, parking), but franchising enforcement ties back to national rules.

LGU ordinances: what they can and cannot do

Can do:

  • Require business permits, regulate loading/unloading, impose safety gear rules within reason, designate parking/terminals, and coordinate traffic flow.
  • Collaborate with DOTr/LTFRB on pilot implementation (e.g., designate corridors) and share data.

Cannot do:

  • Create a local two-wheel PUV franchise in lieu of LTFRB.
  • Authorize routes/fares for two-wheel motorcycles as if they were tricycles or taxis.
  • Contradict national statutes and DOTr/LTFRB directives.

Compliance pathways (today)

  1. Operate only under the DOTr pilot

    • Join an admitted platform or any subsequently authorized operator; follow caps, areas, fare rules, training, equipment, and insurance requirements.
    • Stay strictly within geofences and program terms to avoid colorum liability.

  2. Await or support national legislation

    • When enacted, it should create a clear MC-for-hire class, CPC processes, and long-term safety/insurance frameworks.

  3. If you’re an LGU

    • Issue traffic/safety ordinances that complement national rules; do not attempt to franchise two-wheel services.
    • Coordinate with DOTr/LTFRB for any pilot coverage and data-sharing.

Common questions

Is habal-habal illegal everywhere?

  • As a general PUV service, yes—unless the specific trip is covered by the DOTr pilot (authorized operator, rider, unit, and corridor) and the rider complies with all program conditions.

Can an LGU legalize habal-habal by ordinance?

  • No. LGUs lack authority to grant CPCs for two-wheeled passenger services. They can regulate traffic and permits, but franchising remains national.

Do passengers have insurance?

  • CTPL exists but is limited. In the pilot, platforms provide additional insurance; outside it, coverage for paying passengers may be insufficient, increasing risk exposure.

Are fares regulated?

  • Within the pilot, fares are government-approved/capped. Outside it, fare collection itself may evidence unauthorized PUV operation.

What full legalization would likely require

  • Statutory amendment recognizing motorcycle-for-hire as a PUV class;
  • Clear LTFRB franchising rules (CPC issuance, revocation, penalties);
  • Technical/safety standards (vehicle specs, age limits, inspections, mandatory PPE);
  • Training & accreditation (skills, defensive riding, customer care);
  • Insurance minima for riders and passengers;
  • Data & audit obligations (trip logs, incident reporting, privacy controls);
  • Fare policy and consumer protection (receipts, no overcharging, redress mechanisms);
  • Harmonized LGU coordination (terminals, loading zones, local traffic management).

Practical checklist (for pilot-covered riders & operators)

  • Valid DL with appropriate restriction; clean LTO record.
  • Motorcycle meets TWG specs; maintenance logs up to date.
  • Helmet (ICC/PS marked) for rider & passenger; reflective vest/ID.
  • Phone mount positioned to avoid distraction; no device use while moving.
  • Insurance: CTPL + platform personal accident proof.
  • In-app compliance: onboarding complete, training passed, account active.
  • Operate only in authorized areas/hours, observe fare caps, and keep trip records.

Closing note

As of mid-2024, there is no permanent national law authorizing habal-habal/motorcycle taxis as a PUV class; only the DOTr pilot provides a narrow pathway to operate legally. If you plan to operate, partner, or regulate in this space, align strictly with the pilot and monitor congressional developments for the eventual statutory framework.
